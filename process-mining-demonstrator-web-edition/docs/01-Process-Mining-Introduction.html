Process Mining · Introduction

What is Process Mining?

Your systems already know how your processes really run — they log every step of every order, ticket and claim. Process mining turns those logs into a living flowchart of reality: discovered from data, not drawn from memory.

Process mining reconstructs and analyses business processes from the digital traces ("event logs") that IT systems record as a side effect of normal work. This introduction follows one running example — an online bookstore, from login to delivery (and the occasional return) — to show the principles. They apply unchanged to any process that leaves timestamps behind.
1

Everything starts with an event log

Whenever a customer clicks through an online bookstore, the shop's systems quietly write it all down: who (an order), did what (a step), and when (a timestamp). Process mining needs nothing more than these three columns — data that almost every IT system already records.

An event log: each row is one thing that happened to one order. The rows of different orders interleave — sorting by order and time untangles them.

The three essentials. A case ID (which order / customer journey), an activity (what happened) and a timestamp (when). Extra columns — payment method, customer segment, order value — enrich the analysis later.
2

Discovery — the data draws the flowchart

Sort each order's events by time and you get its journey: the exact route that order took through the shop. Count how often each step follows another across thousands of journeys, and a flowchart emerges — nobody drew it; it was mined from the data. That is process discovery.

Login 12,480→ Browse Catalog 9,062→ Add to Basket 7,911→ Checkout 7,388→ Order Confirmed

Each arrow carries a number: how many journeys took it. The picture shows the process as it really ran — not as a workshop whiteboard imagined it.

  • The map reflects reality, including routes nobody designed on purpose.
  • Rare paths appear thin, common paths thick — the main flow is visible at a glance.
  • Re-mine tomorrow and the map updates itself: it is always as current as the data.
3

Reality has more paths than the ideal process

On the whiteboard, every customer logs in, picks a book, pays and receives it. The mined map tells a richer story: customers loop between browsing and book details, payments fail and are retried, orders come back as returns. These deviations are not noise — they are usually where the money leaks.

Select Payment Method→ Payment Processing 18 %→ Payment Failed → Payment Retry ↩

A loop the designed process never mentioned: failed payments circling back through retry. In the bookstore data, bank-transfer payments fail far more often than credit cards — the map makes that visible immediately.

The happy path. The intended ideal sequence is often called the happy path. Comparing it with the mined reality shows exactly how many journeys follow the plan — and where the rest branch off.
4

Numbers on the map: frequencies and times

Every arrow can be read with different questions in mind: how many journeys took it, what share of all journeys that is, and how long the step-to-step transition took on average, at minimum, at maximum. Slow arrows are bottlenecks; thick arrows into failure steps are systematic problems.

# Count% Journey % ⏱ Avg Time⌄ Min ⌃ Max〰 Std Dev
Warehouse Packing avg 2.1 days→ Shipped avg 1.4 days→ Delivered

The same map, read through a time lens: 2.1 days from packing to shipping is where this bookstore's delivery promise is lost — not at the carrier.

  • Frequency metrics answer “where do journeys actually go?”
  • Time metrics answer “where do they wait?”
  • Spread (min/max/std dev) answers “is it reliably slow, or unpredictably slow?”
5

Conformance — compare reality with the plan

Once the intended process is written down as a reference sequence, every mined journey can be scored against it: does it follow the plan, skip steps, add extra ones? The share of conforming journeys becomes a single, trackable number — and the non-conforming ones can be isolated and inspected.

Checkout→ Enter Shipping Address→ Select Payment Method→ Payment Confirmed
✅ 71 % conform ⚠️ 24 % retry loops ❌ 5 % abandon at payment

Conformance turns “the process feels messy” into measurable shares that can be tracked over time.

6

From insight to improvement — and around again

Process mining is a loop, not a report. In the bookstore: the mined map exposed the bank-transfer failure loop and the packing delay. After fixing both — a better payment provider, an extra packing shift — the next mining run proves whether it worked, on the same data the systems record anyway.

Discover→ Analyse→ Improve→ Monitor↩

Because the evidence is the event log itself, every improvement claim can be verified with the next run.

  • Filter to a segment (only bank-transfer orders, only Premium customers) and the map redraws for exactly those journeys.
  • Drill into a suspicious region of the process without losing the overall picture.
  • Track conformance and cycle times release by release.

Good to know

  • Three classic disciplines. Discovery (mine the map from the log), conformance checking (compare reality with the intended process) and enhancement (extend the map with times, costs and other data).
  • It works on any process that leaves timestamps. Orders, support tickets, patient pathways, insurance claims, IT incidents — if a system logs case, activity and time, it can be mined.
  • The entry cost is low. No new sensors, no interviews, no workshops to reconstruct the process from memory: the raw material already sits in the systems' databases.

The core promise of process mining: decisions about processes stop being debates about opinions, because the process itself — as recorded — is on the table.
